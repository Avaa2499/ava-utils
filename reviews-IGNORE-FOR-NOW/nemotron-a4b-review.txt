However, because the plan is so ambitious and restrictive, the primary feedback isn't about *what* you've done, but about **complexity vs. gain** and **implementation friction**.

---

### 🟢 Strengths (What to preserve)

1. **Fault Isolation:** The commitment to one process per module and no direct dependency is textbook resilient architecture. This is the single biggest strength.
2. **Evolutionary Design:** Defining changes via manifests and communication contracts (in `ava-utils-config`) rather than code coupling is brilliant. This solves the "breaking change" problem elegantly.
3. **Idempotency/State:** Handling missed events and providing current state on connect is critical and often overlooked.
4. **Strict Rules:** The "Rules for every module" section is a powerful design constraint that enforces consistency and prevents common distributed system anti-patterns.
5. **Systemd Integration:** Using socket activation is the correct, idiomatic, and robust way to handle this on Linux, ensuring resource efficiency (nothing running until needed).

### 🟡 Risks & Areas of Friction (What to watch out for)

1. **High Operational Overhead:** While the *system* is robust, the *developer/operator* experience is complex. Everything requires a manifest, a specific install/cycle check, a systemd unit, and specific local state management. Debugging a complex interaction (e.g., `cli` → `file-searcher` → `file-indexer` event) requires tracing through manifests, systemd logs, socket connections, and local state files. **This high barrier to entry might slow early development significantly.**
2. **Latency Tax:** Every interaction requires socket connection setup, encoding/decoding (MessagePack via `ava-utils-config`), and a retry/deadline logic. While these are milliseconds, the sheer volume of necessary setup (manifest checking, cycle checking) adds non-trivial overhead.
3. **The "One Shared Crate" Risk:** `ava-utils-config` is doing *too* much (talking code, message definitions, file-location messages, CLI flag reading). If this crate becomes large, complex, or has a bug, it becomes a critical path dependency for *every single module* during compilation, testing, and deployment. **Keep this crate minimal and strictly interface-oriented.**

### 💡 Recommendations & Focus Areas

1. **Prioritize the Minimum Viable Interaction:** Your first goal (1 hour) is setting up the graph. Your next goal (weekend) is `ava-utils-config` and the manifest/cycle check. **Do not build the first functional module until the manifest/cycle check is rock solid.** This ensures the *governance* of the system works before the *functionality* does.
2. **Simplify Early Interaction:** While your full retry/state logic is perfect, for **Stage 1** (the first three modules), consider simplifying the connection logic slightly. Focus on successful, synchronous request/response first. Introduce the complex retry/state recovery *after* the core mesh interaction is stable.
3. **Validate the Manifest/Install Process:** This is your system's brain. Ensure the install/cycle check is fast and deterministic. A slow install/check will discourage developers from committing frequently (which you correctly mandate).
4. **Address the `au` CLI Scope:** The CLI is currently doing too much (parser, help, raw message output). Ensure it remains purely a *messenger* and *router*, not a business logic handler. Its job is to translate human input into a standardized run-command message and display the resulting text response.
5. **Define the Success Metric:** What is the smallest, simplest, end-to-end use case that proves the architecture works? (e.g., `au file-watcher watch-set-roots /tmp` $\rightarrow$ `file-watcher` sends `files-changed` event $\rightarrow$ `file-indexer` receives event $\rightarrow$ `file-searcher` successfully looks up the index). **Validate this loop before tackling video playback.**
